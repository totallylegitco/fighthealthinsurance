Health coverage in America, by the numbers

Who has coverage, how often insurers say no, how often people push back and win, and what care costs, from government and insurer records. The clearest record of denials comes from HealthCare.gov, where insurers report each year how many claims they deny: about one in five in 2024, and very few are appealed.

The trend

More denials, and in 2024 fewer appeals won

HealthCare.gov plans, all claims (in and out of network), by the year the claims were made. The number of denials more than doubled from 2020 to 2024 as enrollment grew, and the share denied rose from 17% in 2021 to about 20%. In 2024 insurers reversed about a third of the internal appeals people filed, down from 44% the year before. The states and insurers that report change a little each year, and rates aren't adjusted for the kinds of care people used.

Why claims get denied

"Not medically necessary" is a small share of the reasons given

Reasons insurers reported for denied in-network claims in 2024, as a share of all reasons reported. A claim can be counted under more than one reason, so these are shares of reasons, not of claims. "Other" is the largest group, which means many denials aren't explained in this data.

Asking before care

Insurers now post how often they deny prior authorization, and the spread is wide

Prior authorization is when a plan has to approve care before you get it. Since March 2026, Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid plans and HealthCare.gov insurers have had to post what share of 2025 requests they denied. We gathered these from insurers' and states' own websites. Big insurers post one report per contract or state, and we show the largest we found. Insurers count differently, so compare within one kind of plan.

Appeals work

When a denial reaches independent doctors in California, patients usually win

California publishes every Independent Medical Review since 2001. That's the step after an insurer's own appeal, when outside doctors who don't work for the insurer decide. In 2025 they overturned 72% of the denials they reviewed, up from 47% in 2017. These are cases people chose to take that far, and that qualified, so the rate isn't the chance that any denial would be reversed.

Coverage and health

Who has coverage, and how healthy each state is

Uninsured rates for people under 65 (Census, 2024) and adult health measures (CDC, mostly from 2023 surveys). States with higher uninsured rates also tend to have more adults in fair or poor health (correlation 0.52). The link is weaker for diabetes (0.36) and absent for depression. This compares states, not people, and doesn't show that one causes the other.

What you can do

A denial is a first answer, not a final one

Find out why

Ask your insurer for the specific reason and the documents they used. They have to give you the claim file for free. If the reason is paperwork or coding, a corrected claim may fix it without an appeal.

Appeal inside the plan

Most plans give you at least 180 days. In 2024, HealthCare.gov insurers reversed about a third of the internal appeals people filed, and only about 1 in 380 denials led to an appeal.

Ask for independent review

If the plan says no again, most denials about medical judgment can go to outside doctors, through your state or the federal process. In California, independent reviewers sided with patients in 72% of the cases they decided in 2025.

What this can't show yet

The biggest plans report the least

Most Americans get coverage through an employer, and no public dataset reports those plans' denials. That gap is part of the story.

Sources and method
  1. Denials and appeals: CMS Transparency in Coverage public use files, plan years 2022–2026, which report claims from 2020–2024. Individual-market medical plans only, in states using HealthCare.gov. Numbers are FHI's totals of issuer-level figures, not CMS's own, and match KFF's 2024 analysis. Cells CMS suppresses for small counts are left out. Appeal win rates count only insurers that reported both appeals filed and appeals won. Denial reasons are reported per plan for in-network claims, and one claim can count under several reasons, so they're shown as shares of reasons.
  2. Independent medical review: California DMHC IMR determinations, via the CalHHS Open Data Portal, 2010–2025, with exact duplicate records removed.
  3. Prior authorization: each insurer's or state's own 2025 report, posted under the CMS prior authorization rule, copied as posted with a link to the source for every row. Reports whose scope or units weren't clear are left out.
  4. Uninsured: Census Small Area Health Insurance Estimates, under 65, 2023 and 2024.
  5. Health measures: CDC PLACES, 2025 release (mostly 2023 BRFSS). Adult crude prevalence, county estimates weighted by adult population. Kentucky and Pennsylvania aren't in this release.
  6. Drug spending: CMS Medicare Part D Spending by Drug, 2020–2024. Gross spending: what plans and patients paid together, before manufacturer rebates.
  7. Today's counter: 2024 marketplace denials (in and out of network) ÷ 366 days, counted up from your local midnight. It spreads a yearly total evenly for illustration; it isn't a count of denials happening today.
