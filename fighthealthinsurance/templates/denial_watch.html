Where claims get denied

Denial rates run from about 7% to 27%, depending on your state

Share of in-network claims (care from doctors, hospitals and pharmacies that contract with the plan) denied by HealthCare.gov insurers in each state, 2024. Grey states run their own marketplace, so their insurers don't report to HealthCare.gov and aren't shown. The trend

More denials, and appeals can still work

HealthCare.gov plans, all claims (in and out of network), by the year the claims were made. The files for 2020 and 2021 don't split the two, so this chart counts both, which puts 2024 at 101 million denials and 20%, a little above the in-network 19%. The number of denials more than doubled from 2020 to 2024 as enrollment grew, and the share denied went from 18% in 2020 to about 20%, after peaking at 21% in 2023. In 2024 insurers reversed about a third of the internal appeals people filed. The states and insurers that report change each year (Georgia and Illinois have since left HealthCare.gov, and insurers that leave stop reporting), and rates aren't adjusted for the kinds of care people used.

Why claims get denied

"Not medically necessary" is a small share of the reasons given

Reasons insurers reported for denied in-network claims in 2024, as a share of all reasons reported. A claim can be counted under more than one reason, so these are shares of reasons, not of claims. "Other" is the largest group, which means many denials aren't explained in this data. "Not medically necessary" is 5.0% of all reasons (4.0 million of 78.6 million); its physical and behavioral parts round to 4.6% and 0.5%.

Asking before care

Insurers now post how often they deny prior authorization, and the spread is wide

Prior authorization is when a plan has to approve care before you get it. Since March 2026, Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid plans, state Medicaid programs and HealthCare.gov insurers have had to post what share of 2025 requests for medical care and equipment they denied. Prescription drugs aren't included. We gathered these from insurers' and states' own websites. This isn't every plan: big insurers post one report per contract or state, and these 89 are the largest we found. AuthDenied and KFF have fuller collections. Insurers count differently, so compare within one kind of plan.

Your plan's clock

Since 2026, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and CHIP plans must answer a prior authorization request within 72 hours if it's urgent, or 7 days if not. Sometimes they can take up to 14 more days. In Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans, a missed deadline counts as a denial you can appeal.

A denial must tell your doctor the specific reason. HealthCare.gov insurers must give the reason too, but they still have 15 days (72 hours if urgent). These rules don't cover prescription drugs, job-based plans, state-run marketplaces or Original Medicare.

From the CMS prior authorization rule and the federal regulations for each kind of plan, checked October 18, 2026.

Appeals work

When a denial reaches independent doctors in California, patients usually win

California's Department of Managed Health Care publishes every Independent Medical Review it has run since 2001. That's the step after an insurer's own appeal, when outside doctors who don't work for the insurer decide. In 2025 they overturned 72% of the denials they reviewed, up from 47% in 2017. These are cases people chose to take that far, and that qualified, so the rate isn't the chance that any denial would be reversed. It isn't directly comparable with the insurers' own appeal results above either: different plans, people and review processes.

Coverage and health

Who has coverage, and how healthy each state is

Uninsured rates for people under 65 (Census estimates for 2024, with margins of error) and adult health measures (CDC, mostly from 2023 surveys). Across 48 states and DC (Kentucky and Pennsylvania have no 2023 estimates), states with higher uninsured rates also tend to have more adults in fair or poor health (correlation 0.52). The link is weaker for diabetes (0.36) and absent for depression. This compares states, not people, and doesn't show that one causes the other.

What care costs

Where the money goes: prescription drugs in Medicare

A Medicare price tag marks a drug whose price Medicare negotiated with the drugmaker. It's the most Medicare drug plans pay for the drug, not your copay: your plan sets your share, based on that price, so ask your plan how it affects you. Xarelto's ends after 2026 because a generic is now sold. Medicare's list of negotiated drugs.

Help with Medicare drug costs

  • A yearly cap. In a Medicare drug plan, or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage, the most you pay for covered drugs is $2,100 in 2026 and $2,400 in 2027. After that you pay nothing for covered drugs for the rest of the year. Discount cards used instead of your plan don't count toward it. How drug costs work.
  • Extra Help. If your income and savings are limited, Medicare's Extra Help helps pay drug plan premiums, deductibles and copays. In 2027 its copays are at most $5.80 for a generic and $14.40 for a brand-name drug. See if you qualify.
  • Monthly payments. The Medicare Prescription Payment Plan spreads what you owe for drugs across the year. It's free, but it doesn't lower your costs, and Medicare says it may not be the best choice if you join after September, so think about it for 2027. To join, contact your plan. How it works.
  • Free advice. Your state's SHIP gives free one-on-one Medicare counseling, including on drug plans: shiphelp.org or 1-877-839-2675.

What you can do

A denial is a first answer, not a final one

1. Find out why

Ask your insurer for the specific reason and the documents they used. They have to give you the claim file for free. If the reason is paperwork or coding, a corrected claim may fix it without an appeal.

2. Appeal inside the plan

Employer and marketplace plans give you at least 180 days. Medicare Advantage gives 65 days from the date on the notice, and Medicaid plans give 60. Your denial letter has your deadline. In 2024, HealthCare.gov insurers reversed about a third of the internal appeals people filed, and there was about 1 appeal for every 320 in-network denials.

3. Ask for independent review

If the plan says no again, most denials about medical judgment can go to outside doctors, through your state or the federal process. In California, independent reviewers sided with patients in 72% of the cases they decided in 2025.

4. Get help writing it

Fight Health Insurance drafts an appeal from your denial letter for free, with the medical evidence and the rules that apply to your plan. Not sure what your denial means? Get it explained.

Who to call

Your appeal goes to your plan first, with the steps in your denial letter. These offices answer questions and take complaints, by kind of coverage.

  • A job-based plan from a private employer (not a government or church plan): the U.S. Labor Department's benefits advisors, at askebsa.dol.gov or 1-866-444-3272.
  • Medicare, including Medicare Advantage and drug plans: 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227, TTY 1-877-486-2048), or Medicare's complaint form for problems with a plan. To fight a denial, appeal with the steps in your notice.
  • A plan you bought yourself, or a job-based plan your employer buys from an insurer: your state's insurance department. Find it in the NAIC's directory.
  • A surprise medical bill: the No Surprises Help Desk, 1-800-985-3059, or CMS's medical bill rights page.
  • Free Medicare counseling: your state's SHIP, at shiphelp.org or 1-877-839-2675.
  • Your state: our state help pages list each state's insurance department, Medicaid office, SHIP and independent review.

What this can't show yet

The biggest plans report the least

Most Americans get coverage through an employer. The Affordable Care Act calls for those plans to report denials too, but federal regulators have never put that into effect. A few states, like Connecticut, publish figures for the plans they regulate, which leaves out employers' self-funded plans. That gap is part of the story.

Employer plans

About half the country. Federal law calls for denial reporting, but regulators have never put it into effect.

Medicaid and Medicare Advantage

No claim-denial data by state. The prior-authorization reports above cover requests made before care, a different step from claims.

Daily numbers

No public source reports denials more often than once a year. The counter above spreads a yearly total evenly; it isn't a live feed.

Procedure prices

Hospital price files are public but messy, and the standard procedure names are licensed. A future cost explorer would use plain-language names.

Where the numbers come from

Sources and method

  1. Denials and appeals: CMS Transparency in Coverage public use files, plan years 2022 to 2026, which report claims from 2020 to 2024. Individual-market medical plans only, in states using HealthCare.gov. Insurers report these figures to CMS themselves. Numbers are FHI's totals of issuer-level figures, not CMS's own, and closely match KFF's 2024 analysis (KFF leaves out insurers with 1,000 or fewer claims, so it counts 39 fewer appeals). The headline figures, the state map and the insurer table count in-network claims, as KFF does: 84.5 million denials in 2024, 19% of in-network claims, and 263,021 internal appeals, about 1 for every 320 of those denials. The trend chart and its table count all claims, in and out of network (101 million denials, 20%, in 2024), because the files for 2020 and 2021 don't split the two. Cells CMS suppresses for small counts are left out. Appeal counts aren't split by network, so the 1-in-320 figure compares two totals, as KFF's does. Appeal win rates count only insurers that reported both appeals filed and appeals won, and an appeal won in part counts as won. Nine insurers' 2024 appeals-won counts look like reporting errors, so we leave them out too. Four reported none of their 346 to 1,813 appeals won, after winning a quarter to nearly half the year before. For three, CMS marked the win rate as suppressed but printed the counts anyway. Two reported exactly the same counts as in 2023. Their appeals still count as filed. With them, the 2024 rate would be 33.6%. Denial reasons are reported per plan for in-network claims, and one claim can count under several reasons, so they're shown as shares of reasons; like KFF, we leave out CMS's out-of-network reason.
  2. Independent medical review: California DMHC IMR determinations, via the CalHHS Open Data Portal, 2010 to 2025 (file as published June 2026), with exact duplicate records removed.
  3. Prior authorization: each insurer's or state's own 2025 report, posted under the CMS prior authorization rule, copied as posted with a link to the source for every row. Reports whose scope or units weren't clear are left out.
  4. Prior authorization deadlines: the CMS prior authorization rule (CMS-0057-F, February 2024) and the regulations for each kind of plan: 42 CFR 422.122, 422.568 and 422.572 (Medicare Advantage), 440.230 (state Medicaid), 438.210 and 438.404 (Medicaid plans), 457.495 (CHIP) and 45 CFR 156.223 (HealthCare.gov insurers). Medicaid plans' deadlines start with their first contract year beginning on or after January 1, 2026. A state-run CHIP program may follow its state's existing prior authorization law instead. CMS proposed adding prescription drugs in April 2026; we found no final rule. Checked October 18, 2026.
  5. Uninsured: Census Small Area Health Insurance Estimates, under 65, 2023 and 2024.
  6. Health measures: CDC PLACES county data, 2025 release (mostly 2023 BRFSS). Adult crude prevalence, county estimates weighted by adult population. Kentucky and Pennsylvania have no 2023 estimates in this release. Use of CDC data does not imply CDC endorsement.
  7. Drug spending: CMS Medicare Part D Spending by Drug, 2020 to 2024. Gross spending: what Medicare, plans and patients paid together, before manufacturer rebates. CMS marks some names with an asterisk where spending per dose mixes routes of administration; no per-dose figure is shown here, so the mark is left off. The short "for" label next to each drug is its main approved use, checked against MedlinePlus or the drug's FDA label; many drugs have other uses too.
  8. Medicare prices: CMS's selected drugs and negotiated prices, with its fact sheets for 2026, 2027 and 2028. CMS removed Xarelto from the list from January 1, 2027, because a generic is marketed, and is due to publish the 2028 prices by November 30, 2026. Plans' payments can't exceed the negotiated price, and your share is based on it (CMS guidance). Ozempic's price also covers Rybelsus and Wegovy. Checked October 18, 2026.
  9. Medicare drug help: the out-of-pocket cap from CMS's 2027 rate announcement (table V-2) and Medicare.gov's pages on drug costs, Extra Help and the Prescription Payment Plan. Checked October 18, 2026.
  10. Today's counter: 2024 in-network marketplace denials divided by 366 days, counted up from your local midnight. It spreads a yearly total evenly for illustration; it isn't a count of denials happening today.
